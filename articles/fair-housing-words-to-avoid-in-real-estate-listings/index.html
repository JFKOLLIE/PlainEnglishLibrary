Fair Housing words to avoid in real estate listings, and what to write instead

By Plain English Library · Updated September 26, 2026

The rule is simple to state and easy to break in a hurry: describe the property, not the people you imagine living in it. Here is what the law actually says, which phrases HUD has said are fine, and a swap list you can use today.

What the law says, in one paragraph

Under the federal Fair Housing Act, it is unlawful to make, print or publish any notice, statement or advertisement for the sale or rental of a dwelling that indicates a preference, limitation or discrimination because of race, color, religion, sex, handicap (disability), familial status or national origin. The HUD rule that implements this, 24 CFR 100.75, covers written and spoken statements alike, and it specifically names words, phrases, photographs and even the choice of where you advertise.

Many states and cities add more protected classes. The National Fair Housing Alliance lists examples such as sexual orientation, marital status, source of income and use of Housing Choice Vouchers. Check your state and local rules as well as the federal list.

Phrases that clearly cross the line

These state or strongly imply who should or should not live in a home. HUD's 1995 advertising guidance and the NFHA give examples like these:

  • Race, color or national origin: "white family home," "no Irish," "English speakers only."
  • Religion: "Christian home," "no Jews."
  • Disability: "no wheelchairs."
  • Familial status: "no kids," "adults only," or a preference for "couples" or "singles."
  • Source of income, where it is protected locally: "Section 8 not accepted."

Phrases HUD has said are fine

Agents often over-correct and strip useful property descriptions out of their copy. HUD's January 1995 memo on advertising under Section 804(c) told its own investigators not to file complaints over facially neutral descriptions such as:

  • "master bedroom," "family room," "mother-in-law suite," "bachelor apartment"
  • "great view," "walk-in closets," "fourth-floor walk-up," "jogging trails," "walk to bus stop"
  • "two-bedroom," "cozy," "quiet streets," "desirable neighborhood," "rare find"
  • "wheelchair ramp" and other descriptions of accessibility features

That memo is old guidance, not a safe harbor, and your MLS or brokerage may be stricter. When in doubt, follow the stricter rule.

The gray zone: words that describe people, not places

Most trouble in practice comes from friendly-sounding phrases that describe an imagined buyer. They are not on any banned list word for word, but they signal a preference. Rewrite them into facts:

Instead ofWrite
"Perfect for a growing family""Four bedrooms and a fenced backyard"
"Great starter home for young couples""Two bedrooms, kitchen updated in 2021"
"Safe neighborhood""Sidewalks on both sides, street lights, 0.3 miles to the park"
"Walking distance to St. Mary's Church""Half a mile to Main Street shops and transit"
"Ideal for retirees" (unless it qualifies as housing for older persons)"Single-level living with a no-step entry"
"Top-rated schools""Assigned schools are listed on the district website; buyers should verify"

The pattern is always the same: replace a judgment about people with a fact about the property, a measured distance, or a pointer to a source the buyer can check.

Why AI makes this worse, and how to fix it

AI writing tools learned from millions of old listings, so they reach for exactly the phrases above. "Perfect for families" and "safe, friendly neighborhood" show up constantly in first drafts. You are responsible for the ad regardless of who, or what, wrote it. Run every AI draft through a scan before it goes anywhere:

Review the listing copy below for Fair Housing risk under the U.S. Fair Housing Act. Flag any word or phrase that describes people rather than the property, or that suggests a preference based on race, color, religion, sex, disability, familial status or national origin. Also flag claims about safety, crime or school quality. For each flag, quote the phrase, explain the risk in one sentence, and suggest a factual rewrite. Do not rewrite anything that is not flagged.

[paste your listing copy]

The scan is a second pair of eyes, not a compliance review. Read the output yourself, and when something is borderline, ask your broker.

Quick checklist before you publish

  • Every sentence describes the home, the lot, the building or a measurable distance.
  • No mention of who the home is "perfect for" or "ideal for."
  • No claims about safety, crime, demographics or school quality.
  • Photos and ad targeting do not suggest a preferred type of buyer.
  • Your brokerage name and the Equal Housing Opportunity statement or logo appear where your broker requires them.
